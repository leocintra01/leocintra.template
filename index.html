Bemobi — Four themes that should define the story ahead

Bemobi (BMOB3) — [Rating], TP BRL [XX] (YE[XX]). Prices/estimates as of [XX-Sep-2026]; BRL unless noted. Draft for internal review.

Few companies in Brazilian tech have reshaped their revenue mix as quickly as Bemobi, which turned a carrier-billing business into a vertical payments platform for recurring services. Payments now account for ~49% of net revenue (~70% together with SaaS), from ~3% in 2020, and the company serves 16 of the 20 largest recurring-service companies in the country. In this report, we focus on four themes that, in our view, should define the story ahead: i) the pace of TPV growth in payments; ii) the margin trajectory, balancing operating leverage against mix and take-rate pressure; iii) the return on invested capital, which we split between the consolidated business and payments; and iv) capital allocation. Payments: TPV growth continues to accelerate

Organic TPV growth has accelerated even as the base has become larger. TPV reached BRL 3.9 billion in 2Q26 (+54% YoY, or +36% organically), from BRL 3.5 billion in 1Q26 (+48% YoY, +34% organically) and BRL 10.9 billion in 2025, with organic growth accelerating from 26% in 1Q25 to 36% in 2Q26. Payments revenue expanded 34% organically in the quarter, broadly in line with TPV, indicating that the acceleration is volume-driven rather than price-driven.

Most of this growth still comes from the maturation of the existing client base rather than from new logos. Digital penetration within signed accounts remains low, with only ~13% of the invoices of the company's largest client currently paid through digital channels, which leaves a multi-year runway inside contracts that have already been won. In this context, the 2025-26 cohort, which added health, sanitation, higher education and condominiums, only began contributing during 2026 and is not yet fully reflected in the run-rate.

Margins: operating leverage on one side, mix and take-rate on the other

Operating leverage is already visible in the numbers, before the AI agenda matures. The consolidated EBITDA margin reached 34.9% in 2Q26 (+70 bps YoY) and the organic margin reached 36.8% (+2.7 p.p.), with organic operating expenses rising only 3% YoY against organic revenue growth of 15%, reflecting the dilution of the headcount investments made in 2H25. Cash conversion (EBITDA less capex) reached 81.5% (+6.2 p.p.), with capex broadly stable and concentrated in capitalized development.

The gross margin comparison, however, requires care, as the base is not like-for-like. The gross margin of 68.1% in 2Q26, versus 73.2% in 2Q25, reflects i) the restatement of personnel costs carried out from 4Q25 onwards and ii) the consolidation of Paytime in the mix, rather than any operational deterioration. In our view, EBITDA margin is therefore the appropriate metric to follow, and part of the SG&A dilution seen in recent quarters is accounting-driven rather than efficiency-driven. Take-rate is the main counterpoint to the thesis, and the answer lies in volume. The sequential decline in 2Q26 reflects Paytime's lower take rate and a health client whose transactions are concentrated in Pix, while the organic take rate held broadly stable, with payments revenue growing 34% against organic TPV growth of 36%. Looking ahead, the rollout of Pix Automático (already live with five banks and 16 clients) should continue to weigh on monetization, partially offset by higher-value products such as installments, cards and wallets. ROIC: the return of the business and the return of payments

The asset-light model translates into a high return on invested capital. Capex has been running at ~[XX]% of net revenue, mostly capitalized development, and the business requires limited fixed assets to scale, which supports a consolidated ROIC of [XX]% in [XX] against a cost of capital of [XX]%. The return profile of payments, however, differs from that of the consolidated business. At the consolidated level, the goodwill from the acquisitions completed since the IPO weighs on capital employed, diluting the reported return. In payments, capital employed is essentially working capital (gateway receivables against amounts payable to billers), with collection from the end customer over roughly 3.5 months against settlement to the biller in D+30, no credit risk retained and remuneration embedded in the take rate. Our calculation separates the two and compares both with the cost of capital.

Capital allocation: growing, returning cash and acquiring at the same time

Bemobi has been funding growth, shareholder remuneration and acquisitions simultaneously out of its own cash generation. The company returned BRL 60 million to shareholders in 2Q26 (BRL 44 million of buybacks and BRL 16 million of interest on capital) and paid the first installment of the Paytime acquisition, with the cash position declining only BRL 21 million in the quarter, to BRL 328 million. Taken together with the commitment to distribute 100% of 2026 net income, this brings total returns to shareholders to BRL 467 million since the IPO, or roughly 55% of the net proceeds raised in the offering.

The buyback also reduced the volatility of the financial result. The share repurchase carried out in May materially reduced the size of the swap position, which should lower the noise in that line in the coming quarters, while the lower financial income in the quarter (BRL 8.6 million, from BRL 12.7 million) follows from the smaller cash position rather than from the operation. Looking ahead, the decision to fund installments on the balance sheet or through a receivables fund is one of the levers that would free up capital and change the return profile of the payments business.
